Late in 1959, a flatbed truck crawled through evening traffic,
most likely in Mexico City.
On its back was a wooden crate nearly 20 feet long.
Inside was a Soviet moon rocket.
The driver thought he was heading to a rail yard.
He never got there.
At the last turnoff, the truck pulled over.
A canvas was thrown over the crate.
A new man climbed behind the wheel.
And the original driver was led away to a hotel room,
where he would spend the night.
The CIA had just stolen a piece of the Soviet space program.
Now it had one night to take it apart,
photograph every piece and put it all back
before a Soviet official arrived at 7 the next morning to check it in.
This is the story of how they did it.
To understand why the CIA would take that risk,
you have to go back two years.
In October 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik,
the first artificial satellite.
Then the race moved to the moon,
and in 1959, the Soviets won almost every round.
In January, Luna 1 became the first spacecraft to escape Earth's gravity.
In March, America's Pioneer 4 tried to photograph the moon,
but it passed about 37,000 miles away, too far for its camera to work.
In September, Luna 2 crashed into the lunar surface,
the first human-made object ever to reach it.
And on October 4, exactly two years after Sputnik,
Luna 3 lifted off and sent back the first images of the moon's far side.
Western newspapers called these Soviet probes Lunik.
American intelligence could track the launches and intercept the radio signals.
But no American had ever laid hands on the hardware.
Nobody knew which factories built it,
how it was put together, or how many existed.
Then the Soviets did something no one expected.
They sent one on a world tour.
In 1959, Moscow sent a huge exhibition
of its industrial and economic achievements to several countries.
There was heavy machinery, displays on power stations and nuclear equipment,
and a version even came to New York that summer.
The star attractions were the space vehicles, a Sputnik, and a Lunik.
Technically, the Lunik wasn't a satellite.
It was the upper stage of a moon rocket, freshly painted,
set on a pedestal, with three small windows cut into its nose
so visitors could look inside.
Most American analysts assumed it was a mock-up.
No government would parade its real space hardware in front of foreign spies.
But a few analysts weren't convinced.
So at one stop on the tour, after the exhibition closed,
a group of U.S. intelligence officers quietly got access to the Lunik for about 24 hours.
They measured it, traced its wiring, and studied its structure.
The engine and most of the electronics had been removed.
But what remained was no prop.
It was a real, production-built, Soviet space vehicle.
The problem was that one day wasn't enough.
The officers had copied some markings, but not enough to work out who built it.
They needed a second, much longer look.
And the next time, the Soviets would be watching.
The CIA tracked the Lunik the way it tracked most things, through paperwork.
An intercepted shipping manifest listed a crate of models of
astronomic apparatus, with dimensions almost identical to the Lunik's.
The station nearest its next stop was told to get ready.
The CIA has never officially confirmed where that was.
But the most detailed reporting on the operation,
by journalist Jeff Maich in MIT Technology Review, places it in Mexico City.
There, the station was run by Winston Scott, a former FBI cryptographer from Alabama.
According to Maich, in late October 1959,
Scott called a 30-year-old officer from Mexico's secret police into the U.S. embassy.
His name was Eduardo Diaz Silvetti.
By Silvetti's own telling, Scott described the mission as vital to the United States
and warned that if it went wrong, it could start World War III.
On November 19th, two of Scott's officers, his deputy Warren Dean
and a case officer named Robert Zambranardi,
reportedly watched a Soviet ship unload at the port of Tampico.
The crates went onto a train under guard bound for the capital.
Two days later, on November 21st,
the Soviet exhibition opened at the auditorio Nacional.
According to Maich, more than a million people came through its doors.
But this time, the Soviets weren't taking chances.
They posted their own guards on the exhibit 24 hours a day.
The man reportedly responsible for protecting the lunatic
was a Soviet diplomat named Boris Kolomiyakov,
who, according to Maich, was also a KGB officer.
Breaking into the hall at night was impossible.
So the Americans stopped looking at the exhibition
and started looking at what would happen after it closed.
They found the weak spot at the end of the line.
When the show ended, every exhibit would be crated,
loaded onto trucks, and driven to a rail yard.
There, a single Soviet checker would log each crate as it arrived.
The CIA learned that this man had no way to contact his colleagues
back at the exhibition grounds,
and he had no schedule telling him when each truck should arrive.
Intercepting the train was ruled out.
The station had no good way to quietly divert a freight car.
That left the truck.
The plan was simple to describe and nearly impossible to pull off.
Make sure the lunatic was the last truck to leave.
Take it on the way to the rail yard.
Hide it somewhere overnight.
Take it apart.
Photograph everything.
Put it back together and deliver it before the checker returned in the morning.
For the job, the CIA flew in four specialists
from what Finer's report calls the Factory Marketing Center.
Their expertise was reading the stamps, labels,
and serial numbers on enemy equipment.
According to Maish, they arrived posing as vacationers.
One of them was a 29-year-old Russian speaker named Sidney Wesley Finer.
Sam Bernardi worked out what the photographers would need,
including a flash that could recharge fast.
The team bought local clothes so they wouldn't stand out.
Then they bought ladders, ropes, drop lights, a pinch bar, metric wrenches, and a nail puller.
Station officers photographed the crate to work out how it was built.
It was bolted from the inside, so the only way in would be through the roof.
On the day the exhibits were moved, the lunatic was held back
until it was the last truckload to leave the grounds.
Silvetti later claimed his men had helped make sure of that.
In his account, they had filed down a part in the truck's engine.
So it wouldn't start until a replacement arrived around 5 in the evening.
That detail comes only from Silvetti, and some of his story has been disputed.
When the truck finally pulled out, one CIA car drove ahead of it and another followed behind.
Their job was to see whether the Soviets were escorting their rocket.
They weren't.
At the last possible turn-off before the rail yard, the truck stopped.
A canvas went over the crate.
A new driver took the wheel.
The original driver was escorted to a hotel room and kept there for the night.
From that moment on, the operation was on borrowed time.
If anyone on the Soviet side went looking for that truck, there would be nothing to find.
At the rail yard, the Soviet checker waited for more trucks.
None came.
Eventually, he packed up his papers, went to supper, and went back to his hotel.
CIA officers watched him all night in case he came back.
By early evening, a Soviet moon rocket was in American hands,
and no one on the Soviet side knew it was missing.
The truck was driven to a yard surrounded by a 10-foot solid wooden fence.
Finer described it as a salvage yard rented for the night.
Silvetti later said it belonged to his brother-in-law.
At about 7.30 p.m., the four specialists arrived.
A two-man team was already there, handling security and radios.
Before anyone touched the crate, everyone waited for half an hour
to make sure no one had followed the truck.
Then they climbed onto the roof and started pulling up planks,
two-inch boards held down with five-inch spikes.
Partway through, the street lamps around the yard suddenly came on.
The men froze.
For a few seconds, it looked like an ambush.
But no one came.
It was just the city's lights coming on for the night.
Two men lowered themselves into the nose of the rocket.
They took off their shoes so they wouldn't scuff the metal
and began photographing the payload sphere and every marking they could find.
According to Maich, a tarp was stretched over the open roof
so the camera flashes wouldn't light up the sky.
To make sure the camera was working,
a test roll was rushed out by car to be developed.
According to Maich, it went to a dark room at the U.S. Embassy,
and it came back good.
At the other end, two more men took off the base cap,
held on by about 130 bolts.
The engine was gone, but its mounting brackets were still in place,
along with the fuel and oxidizer tanks.
Then they ran into a problem.
The payload sphere was held by a rod,
secured with a wire and a Soviet-stamped plastic seal.
To free the sphere, the wire had to be cut.
And a broken seal was exactly the kind of thing a checker would notice.
They cut it.
Station officers had already said they could reproduce the plastic, the stamp, and the wire.
Before the night was over, a duplicate seal was delivered to the yard.
While they worked, they also found two couplings the Soviets had forgotten to remove.
Those went into a bag bound for Washington.
Taking the rocket apart was the easy part.
Now everything had to go back exactly as it was.
Reattaching the payload sphere took nearly an hour.
A metal plate blocked the men's view, so they had to work largely by feel.
Then the wire was rewrapped and the forged seal fitted.
The team went through every compartment,
checking for anything they might have left behind.
According to Silvetti's account, there was one more scare.
Around four in the morning, the yard went dark.
It turned out to be an ordinary Mexico City blackout.
The roof planks went back on.
At around 4 a.m., the specialists left.
At 5, the truck was taken to a prearranged spot.
There, the original driver, after a night in a hotel room,
got back behind the wheel and drove the rest of the way to the rail yard.
According to Maysh's sources, he had a cover story ready.
He had arrived after the yard closed and waited with the cargo all night.
At 7, the Soviet checker arrived for work.
A crate was waiting for him.
According to Finer's report, he showed no surprise.
He checked it in and watched it loaded onto the train.
For one night, the Soviet moon rocket had been in American hands
and the man responsible for it signed it back in without a second look.
Back in Washington, analysts went through the photographs and markings.
They identified the probable factory that built the rocket stage.
They named three companies that supplied its electrical parts.
They uncovered a system used in this vehicle
and possibly in other Soviet space hardware.
They learned one more thing.
This was the fifth stage of its kind ever produced.
It wasn't a one-off showpiece.
It had come off a production mine.
The CIA later said that knowing how a lunatic was built
helped its analysts judge the rocket's weight, power, and payload limits.
In his report, Finer wrote that there was no sign
the Soviets had ever discovered what happened.
Maysh's reporting suggests they may have found out later,
but no one has established how or when.
The operation stayed secret until 1995, when Finer's report was declassified.
Some of its lines remained blacked out for another 25 years.
Silvetti's fate was harder.
In 1964, Mexico's new president branded him a traitor and he fled to Texas.
And to this day, the CIA has never officially confirmed
which country it all happened in.
Borrowing a Soviet rocket for a single night was bold.
But a few years later, the CIA tried something even more extreme.
It went after a sunken Soviet submarine nearly three miles beneath the Pacific.
That story is next.
